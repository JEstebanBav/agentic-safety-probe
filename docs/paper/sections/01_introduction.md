## 1. Introduction

Large language models have moved beyond systems that only produce text to deployment as agents capable of taking actions in the world. This transition has outpaced understanding of its safety consequences and has left in place an assumption unsupported by the available evidence: that the safeguards obtained by evaluating a model in conversation are preserved when that same model acts.

This work examines that assumption from inside the model. Rather than observing only what an agent does when faced with a harmful request, it examines what happens to the internal representation governing its ability to refuse that request when it moves from one operating context to the other.

In this work, *refusal direction* operationally denotes the normalised difference between the activation centroids of harmful and benign instructions in the conversational format. *Projection* is the dot product of an activation with that axis, and *AUROC* measures its ability to order the two classes. These are representational measures; none is equivalent, by itself, to observing a refusal. The *agentic format* studied here combines a system message and simulated tool schemas: it describes a capacity for action without executing real actions.

The study uses a contrastive corpus of 96 harmful–benign instruction pairs and four open-weight models from three families. In addition to internal representations, it examines the models' behaviour without intervention and subjects the relationship between the refusal direction and that behaviour to a causal test.

### 1.1. Problem statement

Over the last two years, large language models (LLMs) have moved from purely conversational systems to deployment as agents: systems capable of executing code, sending emails, querying databases or initiating transactions through external tools, without human intervention at every step. Adoption has been rapid. Industry reports estimate that by the end of 2026 approximately 40% of enterprise applications will incorporate task-specific agents, compared with fewer than 5% two years earlier {{c:miniorange2026}}. What was once experimental has become infrastructure.

This change alters the nature of the risk. A failure in a conversational system produces inappropriate text; a failure in an agentic system produces actions with real effects on data, money or infrastructure {{c:andriushchenko2024}}. Because agentic tasks comprise multiple linked steps, an error at one point propagates through the operation, and tool access amplifies its reach, so the same failure can affect one account or hundreds. This difference is beginning to appear in industry experience. According to one industry report, 88% of surveyed organisations experienced agent-related security incidents in the preceding year, rising to 92.7% in healthcare, with loss of control over agent behaviour identified as the predominant risk {{c:gravitee2026}}. These figures describe industry perceptions and experience rather than controlled measurements, but they are consistent with the academic evidence discussed below.

At the root of this vulnerability is an assumption accepted during deployment: that the safety guarantees obtained by evaluating a model in a conversational format are preserved when the same model operates as an agent. Andriushchenko et al. {{c:andriushchenko2024}} showed that this assumption is false. Through the AgentHarm benchmark, they documented that frontier models perform harmful tasks in an agentic format without requiring evasion techniques, that chat jailbreaks transfer readily to the agentic context, and that a model whose defences have been compromised retains its capabilities, performing the harmful task as competently as a legitimate one. Safety evaluated in one context does not transfer to the other.

This evidence describes the failure without explaining it. It measures that safeguards give way in an agentic context, but not what happens inside the model for them to do so. Approaching that explanation requires examining the internal mechanisms supporting safety. Arditi et al. {{c:arditi2024}} showed that refusal of harmful requests is not diffusely distributed through the network, but is mediated by a single direction in activation space, a finding replicated across thirteen open models with up to 72 billion parameters: removing the direction eliminates refusal, whereas strengthening it induces refusal even of harmless requests. Refusal therefore has a concrete, measurable geometric form causally linked to behaviour. It is not an isolated case: subsequent work identified internal representations for other sensitive behaviours, such as deception or writing unsafe code {{c:templeton2024}}, indicating that model safety depends on a broader set of internal representations rather than one isolated mechanism.

Two aspects of these mechanisms require a specific diagnosis. The first is their sensitivity to how harm is presented. An aligned model readily refuses an openly harmful instruction, but practically relevant cases are often requests that appear legitimate while leaving harmful intent implicit in the context. It is not known whether the internal representation of harmfulness activates equally strongly for explicit and concealed instructions, or whether it weakens as the harm becomes less visible on the surface of the text. The second aspect is the operating context. The refusal direction has been characterised almost entirely in the conversational format {{c:arditi2024}}; its behaviour when the model has tools has received little attention. The closest work, by Lermen et al. {{c:lermen2024}}, removed the refusal vector from a large model and confirmed that the resulting agent completed harmful tasks. However, it eliminated the mechanism by design, without measuring whether that mechanism weakens naturally when the format becomes agentic.

The available evidence therefore leaves a precise question open. Behaviourally, safeguards are known to fail when a model acts as an agent {{c:andriushchenko2024}}, and mechanistically, refusal is known to be mediated by an activation-space direction characterised in the conversational format {{c:arditi2024}}. What has not been established is whether that representation retains its ability to detect harmful intent when the model moves to the agentic format, and whether that ability holds for requests with different levels of explicitness. This is the question addressed here.

### 1.2. Central research question

When a language model moves from conversation to acting as an agent, does its refusal direction retain the ability to detect harmful intent, including when that intent is concealed?

This question brings together two dimensions of the same phenomenon—the robustness of the refusal representation—and both fall within the study's scope. The first is the change from conversational to agentic format. The second is the explicitness with which harmful intent is presented. The contrastive design described in the methodology crosses these factors so that they can be addressed jointly rather than as separate problems.

### 1.3. Rationale and expected value

Understanding what happens to internal safety representations when a model operates as an agent is not solely a theoretical concern. The gap between the speed of deployment and the maturity of safety controls is a critical issue in the use of AI. Industry reports describe this disconnect clearly. Although 82% of executives trust that their current policies prevent unauthorised agent actions, more than half of production agents operate without supervision or logging. This is attributed to organisations applying security frameworks designed for conventional software to agents that make autonomous decisions and invoke tools in ways those frameworks do not anticipate {{c:gravitee2026}}. Predominant controls operate outside the model through permissions, credentials and authorisation limits. The failure relevant here, however, also occurs in the internal decision to execute or refuse an action. A defence that disregards what the model represents internally before acting addresses the symptom rather than the cause.

The behavioural approach that dominates evaluation is insufficient to close this gap. Benchmarks measure what a model does, not why it does so, and this distinction has practical consequences: without an explanation of the mechanism, an isolated failure cannot be distinguished from a structural vulnerability, nor can the conditions under which it will recur be anticipated. A further limitation is that a model may behave differently when it infers that it is being evaluated, imposing a ceiling on methods based only on observing behaviour {{c:greenblatt2024}}. Mechanistic interpretability offers a complementary approach. Examining internal representations and conducting controlled interventions on specific mechanisms can bring us closer to causal explanations of a failure's origin and, from them, to targeted interventions {{c:arditi2024}}. The feasibility of this approach is supported by frontier laboratories' efforts to audit safety representations in their own production models {{c:templeton2024}}; if training-based alignment were sufficient, that effort would be unnecessary.

The refusal direction offers a suitable entry point. It is the safety mechanism most thoroughly characterised mathematically, with a transparent, reproducible calculation method and an already demonstrated causal relationship with behaviour {{c:arditi2024}}. It therefore provides a case in which the method can be validated rigorously before extending it to more complex representations. Diagnosing how this direction changes in the agentic format, and whether its detection capacity withstands harmful intent of differing explicitness, supplies the mechanistic explanation unavailable from behavioural evidence. This diagnosis could also provide a basis for future work on restoring the representation when it degrades. Such restoration lies beyond the primarily diagnostic purpose of the investigation, but could in principle open a protection strategy based on internal representations, applicable to the broader set of safety behaviours encoded by the model and complementary to the external controls that industry recognises as insufficient.

Finally, this line of work belongs to a rapidly expanding field whose Latin American participation remains concentrated in a few countries. According to the Latin American Artificial Intelligence Index, only seven countries in the region participate in the main tracks of elite international conferences, and 82% of the participating regional authors come from Chile and Brazil. This reflects the limited presence of the rest of the region, including Colombia, in frontier research venues {{c:ilia2025}}. Conducting this research in Colombia, with attention to the actual deployment conditions of these systems in the region, contributes both to knowledge of the phenomenon and to diversifying the perspectives shaping the AI safety research agenda.

### 1.4. Research objectives

#### 1.4.1. General objective

To analyse, using mechanistic interpretability techniques, how the refusal direction in a language model's internal representations changes when moving from conversational to agentic format, and to evaluate the stability of its detection capacity according to the explicitness of harmful intent, with a preliminary exploration of its potential as the basis of a safety-monitoring mechanism.

#### 1.4.2. Specific objectives

1. To construct a contrastive dataset that isolates the effect of the agentic format on the model's internal representations. Within each pair, instruction content remains identical between conversational and agentic formats, so the manipulation within the comparison is the format composed of system message and tools. The dataset also stratifies base instructions by harmful-intent explicitness, a dimension that distinguishes pairs from one another and does not introduce variation within a paired comparison.
2. To calculate the refusal direction from model activations and characterise its layer-wise behaviour in the conversational format as a baseline.
3. To quantify the change in projection onto the refusal direction between conversational and agentic formats for identical instructions, assessing the statistical significance of the observed difference.
4. To evaluate the stability of the refusal direction's detection capacity by harmful-intent explicitness, comparing its response to requests with overt and concealed intent.
5. To explore, on a preliminary basis, the feasibility of using the refusal direction as the basis of a monitoring mechanism that detects harmful intent in the agentic format. This exploration assesses its potential rather than developing a functional system.
