## 2. Context and related work

### 2.1. Characterisation of the context

Three dimensions characterise the setting in which the phenomenon studied here occurs. The first is the agentic format as a technical operating environment. The difference between a model that converses and a model that acts does not lie in the model, which is the same, but in what accompanies the instruction within its context. In the agentic format, the model additionally receives a system message that positions it as an agent and a catalogue of tools described in function-calling format, enabling an additional type of output: the invocation of an action that the system will execute on its behalf. Between the response and its effect, the human who, in conversational format, reads the response and decides whether to act disappears. The decision to comply or refuse, made once at the beginning, governs the entire subsequent sequence of actions. For this work, the relevant features are that the agentic stimulus is longer, includes technical vocabulary unrelated to the request, and places the model in a different role. Any of these elements can alter the representations on which the refusal mechanism operates.

The second dimension concerns open-weight models as observable objects. A study of mechanistic interpretability requires access to internal activations during processing, something that systems accessible only through application programming interfaces do not provide. This is why the literature on the refusal direction has been built on open models {{c:arditi2024}}, and why this work requires the models studied to be open, aligned and capable of using tools. This condition introduces a tension that the work does not resolve: the systems whose failures motivate the investigation are, for the most part, closed.

The third dimension is the space of requests. Requests that an aligned model should refuse differ in the type of harm, which this work organises into seven categories taken from the established taxonomy for agent evaluation {{c:andriushchenko2024}}, and in the degree to which harmful intent is manifested on the surface of the text, a dimension introduced here as a construct of the study itself. The latter matters because practical risk is not concentrated where harm is evident: requests that get through safeguards are those whose dangerousness lies in their purpose rather than in their words.

### 2.2. Background and references

#### 2.2.1. Background

##### Alignment as the origin of refusal behaviour

A language model's behaviour of refusing to comply with harmful requests is not a spontaneous property. It is the result of a training process that follows pretraining, known as alignment. Ouyang et al. {{c:ouyang2022}} established that increasing a model's size does not, on its own, make it better at following the user's intent: large models can generate content that is untruthful, toxic or simply unhelpful, that is, content that is not aligned with the user. To correct this misalignment, they proposed a procedure with two stages. In the first, the model is fine-tuned through supervised learning on demonstrations of the desired behaviour written by annotators. In the second, human rankings of the model's outputs are collected, a preference model is trained from those rankings, and that model is used as a reward signal for further fine-tuning through reinforcement learning. This procedure is known as reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF. The resulting model, called InstructGPT, showed improvements in truthfulness and reductions in toxic output generation, to the point that its outputs were preferred over those of a model a hundred times larger without this fine-tuning. RLHF thus provides a route by which a model learns to behave usefully and harmlessly and is an origin of the refusal behaviour examined in this investigation.

Building on this foundation, Bai et al. {{c:bai2022}} proposed a procedure specifically oriented towards harmlessness, called Constitutional AI, which reduces dependence on human labels for identifying harmful content. In their method, human supervision is limited to a set of principles or rules, and the model itself generates self-critiques and revisions of its responses according to those principles. A model subsequently evaluates preferences between responses, and that AI-generated signal replaces the human signal in the reinforcement stage. Beyond the procedure itself, their work contributes a conceptual distinction relevant to this investigation: the desired result is not an evasive model but a harmless, non-evasive assistant capable of engaging with harmful queries by explaining its objections to them. In this sense, refusal is not simple silence in the face of something dangerous, but a deliberate behaviour resulting from alignment.

##### From conversational models to agents and safeguard failure

Refusal behaviour was established and evaluated, for the most part, in models operating in a conversational format. However, the recent deployment of these models as agents, equipped with tools that allow them to act in the world, introduced a setting in which the safeguards obtained through alignment are not necessarily preserved. Andriushchenko et al. {{c:andriushchenko2024}} provided the first systematic measurement of this problem through the AgentHarm benchmark, which evaluates agent behaviour on 110 explicitly harmful tasks distributed across eleven harm categories and performed using simulated tools. Their results showed that frontier models comply with a considerable fraction of these tasks without the need for evasion techniques, that evasion attacks designed for the conversational format transfer effectively to the agentic context, and that a model whose defences have been compromised retains its capabilities, executing the harmful task with the same competence with which it would execute a legitimate one. This work established at the behavioural level that safety evaluated in the conversational format does not transfer to the agentic format. It left open the question of the internal mechanisms responsible for that failure, a question that purely behavioural approaches cannot resolve.

#### 2.2.2. Current state of knowledge

##### Reading internal representations with linear classifiers

Studying the internal mechanisms of a model requires methods capable of extracting interpretable information from its representations. The methodological foundation of this approach goes back to Alain and Bengio {{c:alain2017}}, who proposed using linear classifiers, called *probes*, trained independently of the model on the activations of an intermediate layer, to measure how suitable those activations are for a classification task. A central observation of their work is that the linear separability of features increases monotonically with network depth: information becomes progressively more accessible in a linear way in deeper layers. Although their study concerned computer vision models, the linear-probe technique is the methodological antecedent of the subsequent work that locates concepts in a model's activations, and it is the basis for the measurement of class separability used in this investigation.

##### The linear representation hypothesis and representation engineering

The reason a linear probe is effective is formalised by the linear representation hypothesis, according to which many high-level concepts are encoded as linear directions in a model's activation space. Park et al. {{c:park2024}} developed a formulation of this hypothesis and of the geometry of representation space that supports it, providing the theoretical framework that justifies looking for a concept, such as an instruction's harmfulness, in the form of a direction. On this foundation, Zou et al. {{c:zou2023}} proposed the framework of representation engineering, which systematises the reading and manipulation of high-level concepts in a network's activations and establishes procedures for extracting directions associated with behaviours and intervening on them during inference. This framework supplies the conceptual and technical tools on which the study of specific safety mechanisms, such as refusal, is built.

##### The refusal direction and its auditability

Arditi et al. {{c:arditi2024}} applied these tools to refusal behaviour, demonstrating that refusal of harmful requests is not diffusely distributed throughout the network but is mediated by a single direction in the activation space of the residual stream. Their method, based on the difference between the mean activations of harmful and benign instructions, allows that direction to be extracted, and their interventions establish its causal role: eliminating the direction suppresses the capacity to refuse, whereas adding it induces refusal even for harmless requests. This result, validated in thirteen open models with up to 72 billion parameters, is the direct methodological foundation of the present investigation. In complementary work, Templeton et al. {{c:templeton2024}} showed, using sparse autoencoders applied to a production model, that millions of interpretable features can be extracted from internal representations, including features associated with safety concepts such as deception and unsafe-code generation. Their work shows that safety representations extend beyond the isolated case of refusal and constitute an auditable, widespread phenomenon in a model's internal representations.

##### Refusal under intervention and its contextual dependence

A recent body of work has begun to examine the refusal direction beyond the conversational format in which it was characterised, revealing that it is neither a stable nor an isolated mechanism. Lermen et al. {{c:lermen2024}} applied refusal-vector ablation to a large-scale model and verified that the resulting agent completed harmful tasks that it would have refused without the intervention. Their approach was nevertheless interventionist: it deliberately eliminated the mechanism instead of measuring whether it weakens naturally when context changes. Zhong and Li {{c:zhong2026}}, in turn, showed that the refusal direction depends on the representation of the model's persona. When the model is steered towards a compliant persona, the refusal rate collapses, and reintroducing the refusal direction restores the behaviour only at late layers. This leads them to conclude that refusal is gated at the expression stage, downstream of where it is computed. Treating refusal as a single isolated direction, they point out, overlooks its dependence on context.

The work closest to the present study is that of Pinto {{c:pinto2026}}, who conducted the first systematic analysis of conversational-to-agentic transfer of the refusal direction used to steer behaviour. Its finding is that the direction reaches late layers at near-full strength in every evaluated model, while its coupling to behaviour is recalibrated according to the model and context, being amplified in some cases and attenuated in others. However, that study is oriented towards controlling behaviour by injecting the direction, rather than diagnosing the direction's capacity to detect harmful intent.

### 2.3. Identified research gap

The available evidence therefore leaves a precise point open. It is known at the behavioural level that safeguards fail when a model acts as an agent {{c:andriushchenko2024}}, and at the mechanistic level that refusal is mediated by a direction in activation space characterised in the conversational format {{c:arditi2024}}. What remains undetermined is not, therefore, whether that direction persists during the move to the agentic format—Pinto tracked it for behavioural control and found it almost intact at late layers—but whether, when used as a diagnostic instrument, it retains its capacity to detect harmful intent when the model operates as an agent, and whether that capacity is maintained for requests with different levels of explicitness. This is the question addressed by the present work.

The present investigation differs from these studies in two respects. First, its diagnostic component adopts an observational perspective: it measures how projection onto the refusal direction changes when moving to the agentic format without modifying the model in that analysis. Second, it incorporates the explicitness of harmful intent as an analytical dimension, an aspect not addressed in the preceding literature and central to evaluating the robustness of the refusal mechanism for requests whose harmfulness is not evident on the surface of the text.

The gap thus has two components, which this work addresses jointly. The first is that the refusal direction has been characterised in one operating context, the conversational context, different from the context in which models fail, without measuring what happens to that representation during the transition between the two without intervening on the model. The second is that this characterisation has been built on requests whose harmfulness is explicit, leaving it unknown whether detection capacity is preserved when intent is concealed.

The specific objectives formulated in Section 1.4 respond to this double gap in sequence. The first constructs the instrument that makes it possible to isolate the format effect: a contrastive dataset with identical text across conditions, stratified by explicitness, which no available resource provided. The second establishes the conversational baseline against which the comparison is made. The third quantifies the shift between formats and its significance, addressing the first component of the gap. The fourth evaluates detection stability according to explicitness, addressing the second component. The fifth explores what practical consequences the resulting diagnosis would have for constructing monitoring mechanisms, connecting the mechanistic finding back to the applied problem that motivates it.
